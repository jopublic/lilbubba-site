How to Safely Clean Up Developer Disk Space on Windows (Without Breaking Your Projects)

By LilBuba.ai · 28 July 2026 · 6 min read

If you write code on Windows, your SSD is quietly filling up with things you don't need — and most of it will come straight back the moment you actually need it. The trick to reclaiming that space isn't deleting aggressively. It's deleting the right things, and being able to undo it if you get one wrong.

Where the space actually goes

It's rarely your code. It's the regenerable junk that piles up around it:

  • node_modules — every JavaScript project drags one along, often hundreds of megabytes to gigabytes each. Ten old side-projects and you're into serious space. (We went deep on this one in node_modules Is Eating Your Hard Drive.)
  • Python .venv, __pycache__, .pytest_cache — virtual environments and caches that rebuild in seconds.
  • Build output — build/, dist/, target/, bin/, obj/ — compiled artifacts you can regenerate any time.
  • Package-manager caches — npm, pip, yarn, cargo and NuGet all hoard downloads "just in case".
  • .git bloat — old branches and large-file history in repos you abandoned long ago.

The common thread: all of it is regenerable. Delete a node_modules and npm install brings it back. Delete a build/ and your next compile rebuilds it. It's cache, not work.

Why people don't clean it up (and the one real danger)

Two reasons. First, it's tedious — the junk is scattered across dozens of project folders. Second, and more importantly: it's genuinely scary. Delete the wrong folder and you've binned a config, an .env, an uncommitted change, or a local database you forgot was there. So most of us just… buy a bigger drive.

The safe way to do it has three rules:

  1. Only touch the regenerable stuff — never source, never config, never uncommitted work.
  2. Know an active project from a dead one — the folder you're working in today should be left alone.
  3. Make it reversible — nothing should be truly gone until you're certain.

Doing it by hand (and the free tools that help)

You can absolutely script this yourself. Credit where it's due — there are excellent free command-line tools for it. npkill is brilliant for hunting down node_modules, and kondo cleans a wider set of project artifacts. If you live in the terminal and trust your own rm -rf, they're superb and free, and we'll happily say so.

The catch is they're command-line, they mostly delete outright (no safety net), and they tend to solve one slice of the problem rather than the whole messy picture on a Windows machine.
